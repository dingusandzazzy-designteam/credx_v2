Where the Card Fee Goes on Every Sale

Every card sale carries a fee that leaves the business and does not come back. It is the largest cost most operators never see itemized, and following one transaction through explains why.

26 August 2026 · 4 min read

What is actually deducted?

On a card sale, a share of the amount is deducted before the business ever sees it. Most of that share is interchange, set by the competing credit facility behind the customer's card and collected on the way through. The business does not negotiate it, does not see it broken out on a customer receipt, and cannot decline it — it is a condition of accepting the payment at all.

The figure is small enough per transaction to ignore and large enough per year not to. On roughly $1M of monthly volume it comes to about $36,000 a month leaving the business as interchange. Results vary by volume and card mix, so that is an illustrative figure rather than a quote — but it is the right order of magnitude, and it is a recurring cost with no asset at the end of it.

Who takes a share, and for what?

Each party in the chain is paid for a real service, which is why the arrangement has survived unchallenged for decades. The competing credit facility funded the customer's purchase and carried the risk of not being repaid. The infrastructure that moved the money was built and is maintained. None of that is unreasonable on its own.

What is worth noticing is what the business gets back for the largest share of it. The credit was extended in someone else's name. The relationship the sale created attaches to whoever issued the card, not to the business the customer walked into. And the record of what was bought accumulates somewhere the business cannot reach. The business generated the value and holds the least durable claim on what it produced.

What changes if the credit is funded differently?

If a community lender funds the credit at the business's own checkout, the same transaction settles differently. The purchase price does not change. The business is paid in full and upfront, exactly as it is today, and it does not take the credit risk onto its own books — the lender funds the credit and carries that risk. What changes is where the fee on the sale lands.

That share returning to the business instead of leaving it is what interchange recovery means. It runs from 40% up to 85% of standard interchange, depending on qualifying volume. At the top of that range, roughly $30,000 of every $1M in monthly volume stays in the business; at the lower end the same volume returns closer to $14,000. It is a range rather than a single outcome, and where a given business lands depends on its own volume and mix.

Part of that recovered share can go back out to the customer under the business's own name rather than a third party's. That is what a value-back program means here: the reason to come back belongs to the business that earned the sale.

The second thing that changes is quieter and compounds for longer. The credit carries the business's name, so the reason the customer comes back belongs to the business. And access to the transaction record — on explicit consumer opt-in, under PIPEDA — sits with the business that earned it rather than with a third party that will sell it a report on itself.

Questions.

How much does interchange cost a business per million in volume?

Roughly $36,000 per $1M of monthly volume goes to a competing credit facility as interchange. Results vary by volume and card mix, so treat that as an illustrative figure rather than a quote.

Does recovering interchange mean charging the customer more?

No. The purchase price does not change and the business is paid in full and upfront on every sale, exactly as it is today. What changes is who funds the credit behind the sale, and therefore where the fee on it settles.
