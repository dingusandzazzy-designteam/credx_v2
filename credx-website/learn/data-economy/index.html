The Data Economy Runs on Your Customers’ Data

Every time a customer pays, the transaction leaves a record behind. That record is the raw material of the data economy, and today it is captured, priced and resold by parties that never met the customer who created it.

Where the record goes today

The sale happens inside the business. The point-of-sale system records it, the payment processor clears it, the card network routes it, and the institution behind the customer’s card settles it. Each party keeps what it needs to do its job, and several of them keep rather more than that.

By the time the money lands, the business has the amount and little else. The party holding the fullest picture of the customer is the one furthest from them: it never served them, never met them, and has no stake in whether they come back.

What a single transaction actually produces

A payment is not one fact. It produces several, and they fall into five categories: transaction, what was bought and for how much; demographic, who the buyer is in broad terms; behavioural, how they buy and how often they return; credit and financial, what they can responsibly afford; and lifestyle, the pattern the other four make together.

Most operators only ever see the first, and only for their own counter. The categories that explain why a customer returns are assembled somewhere else, tied to the individual rather than to an aggregate, and sold back to the businesses that generated them in the first place.

Consent is the line that actually matters

There is a difference between data a customer agreed to share and data collected because they happened to pay. The first is a relationship. The second is a by-product, and most of the data economy runs on the second.

CredX is built on the first. Participation is opt-in at every step, what is shared is de-identified, and it is held to Canadian privacy law. A business sees the shape of its own customer base, never a file on a person.

Open loop, closed loop, and who holds the record

In an open loop, the record resolves outward. The transaction is routed through a network that sits above every business it touches, and the intelligence it produces belongs to that network rather than to the counter where the sale happened.

In a closed loop, it resolves inward. The credit runs on a facility the business takes part in, so the record of what its customers do stays with the business that earned it. Same purchase, same checkout. The difference is which direction the information travels afterwards.

What changes when the record stays with you

Three things, and none of them is a report you have to read. You can see which buyers return and which do not, which is a staffing and stock decision before it is a marketing one. You stop paying a third party for access to a picture your own counter produced. And the picture compounds, because every month adds to it rather than renting it back to you.

That is the whole argument for treating the record as an asset of the business. It was always an asset. What the data economy settles is whose.
